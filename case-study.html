Facilitation where the output has to stand up

Echelon is a new practice, so we won’t show you invented client logos. Here is the facilitation experience behind it, why it transfers to leadership teams, and exactly what a session with us looks like.

The Experience Behind Echelon

Large, mixed-expertise rooms that must produce work people will rely on

As Head of Assessment at a leading UK medical school, Dr Andrew Greenland designs and facilitates the workshops that create, test and agree the final exams that decide who qualifies as a doctor. The material has to be accurate, fair and defensible - and it has to be produced on the day, by busy senior experts who don’t always agree.

What those sessions involve

  • Scenario development workshops (around 10 a year, up to 70-80 people): clinicians, academics and actors generate large numbers of realistic clinical scenarios for final exams in a single structured session.
  • Written question workshops (around 8 a year, 20-30 people): small expert groups draft and challenge exam questions until each one is good enough to be used.
  • Standard-setting meetings: experts with different judgements agree a defensible pass mark - a decision that directly affects people’s careers.
  • Piloting sessions: new material is tested before it goes live, and weak items are changed or dropped.
  • Actor training (80-90 participants): a large group is brought to the same understanding so every candidate experiences the scenario in the same way.
Why It Transfers

The same problems, in a different building

Illustrative Example

A one-day Executive Alignment Sprint for a 12-person leadership team

Please note: this is an illustrative example of how we design and run a session. It is not a write-up of a specific client engagement.

The situation

A UK professional services firm of 300-500 staff is mid-way through a restructure. The executive team has held three strategy sessions in six months. Each ended with a slide deck and no binding commitments, and middle managers are getting contradictory direction.

What makes it hard

  • 12 senior leaders across 4 divisions, with real status and political dynamics
  • A CEO who tends to speak first and anchor the room
  • Two division heads with a known tension, and two people joining remotely

How the session is designed

  • Before: a diagnostic call with the CEO and HR lead, and three pre-work questions for every participant
  • Silent first: everyone writes their priorities alone before any discussion, which removes the anchoring effect
  • Trade-offs: for every top priority, the room answers ‘what stops or slows for this to happen?’
  • Commitment: nothing leaves the room without a named owner, a deadline and success criteria
  • Read-back: the full decision log is read aloud and each leader confirms their commitments on record

What the team leaves with

Decision LogEvery decision with options, trade-off, owner, deadline and success criteria
One-Page Priority MapTop priorities ranked and resourced, with what was de-scoped
Stopped Initiatives ListWork formally stopped, with the reason, ready to communicate
30-Day Review FrameworkCheck-in cadence, escalation rules and who calls time on drift
Illustrative Example

From 70 experts in a room to a business-wide working session

Please note: this example shows how the approach used in large assessment workshops would apply to a business. It is not a write-up of a specific client engagement.

The situation

A 60-person senior leadership group meets for a transformation kick-off. Large sessions like this usually produce energy and a long list of ideas - and very little that anyone owns a month later.

How we would run it

  • Structured small groups: mixed tables, each with a clear brief and a template for what a ‘usable’ proposal looks like - the same way exam scenarios are drafted to a standard
  • Built-in challenge: tables swap and stress-test each other’s proposals, as draft exam questions are reviewed before they are accepted
  • Agreeing the bar: the group agrees in advance how proposals will be judged, then scores them - the standard-setting principle
  • One shared understanding: the final priorities are briefed back so every leader can explain them the same way to their teams

What the group leaves with

A short, ranked list of initiatives that met an agreed standard, a named sponsor for each, and consistent messages to take back to the business.
