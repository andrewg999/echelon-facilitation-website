Design Sprint or Decision Workshop? How Leadership Teams Should Choose

Dr Andrew Greenland • 3 October 2026 • 8 min read

Short answer: a design sprint is built to solve a defined problem by prototyping and testing a solution in a few days. A decision workshop is built to get a leadership team to make and own choices - priorities, trade-offs and who decides what. If your team already agrees on the problem and needs a tested solution, use a design sprint. If your team can't agree on the problem, the priorities or who gets to decide, a design sprint will stall, and you need a decision workshop first.

Leadership teams often ask for a 'sprint' when what they actually need is a decision. The two formats share some techniques - silent writing, voting, time-boxing - so they look similar from the outside. But they are designed to produce very different things, and choosing the wrong one wastes a day of your most expensive people.

What is a design sprint?

A design sprint is a structured process, usually run over four or five days (sometimes compressed into two), in which a small team takes one well-defined challenge, sketches possible solutions, picks one, builds a realistic prototype and tests it with real users. It came from product design, and it is very good at what it was built for: reducing the risk of building the wrong product or service.

A design sprint works best when:

  • the challenge is specific, such as a new customer journey, a service or a product feature
  • the team agrees the challenge is worth solving
  • one person (the 'decider') has the authority to choose a direction
  • the output you need is a tested prototype and evidence from users

What is a decision workshop?

A decision workshop is a facilitated session in which a leadership team makes binding choices about direction: what the priorities are, what stops so they can happen, who owns each commitment and who has the right to decide what. The output is not a prototype. It is a written decision log with owners, deadlines and trade-offs, agreed in the room.

A decision workshop works best when:

  • the team has discussed the same issues for months without resolving them
  • priorities compete and nobody has named what will be dropped
  • decisions get made in meetings and unmade afterwards
  • the people in the room have different status, agendas and appetites for risk

Why design sprints stall with leadership teams

Design sprint methods are excellent, and many of their techniques - working alone before discussing, voting before debating, strict time-boxes - are used in good leadership facilitation too. The problems appear when a product-design format is used for a leadership problem:

  • The 'decider' doesn't exist. A sprint relies on one person who can choose. In many leadership teams, the real issue is that nobody has clear authority, or the chief executive's view anchors everyone else.
  • The problem isn't agreed. A sprint starts from a defined challenge. If leaders disagree about what the challenge is, the first two days get spent arguing about the brief.
  • Trade-offs are left unnamed. A sprint chooses one solution to test. A leadership team needs to choose what it will stop doing, which is harder and more political.
  • The output is the wrong shape. A prototype is not something a leadership team can execute against. A decision log with owners and deadlines is.

The two can work together: a decision workshop to agree priorities and decision rights, followed by design sprints on the specific challenges that come out of it.

Specialist facilitator or large consultancy?

For a leadership offsite or strategy day, teams often weigh up an independent specialist facilitator against a large consulting firm. They do different jobs.

  • Large consultancies are strongest when you need analysis, research, benchmarking or people to implement a programme. Workshops are usually one part of a wider engagement, and the firm will often bring its own view of the answer.
  • Specialist facilitators are strongest when the answer is already in the room and the problem is getting senior people to agree, commit and follow through. A good facilitator stays neutral on the content and focuses on how the decision gets made.

Ask yourself whether you are short of information or short of agreement. If you are short of information, you need analysis. If you are short of agreement, you need facilitation.

What about AI strategy workshops?

AI is where the confusion between the two formats shows up most. Many AI workshops are really ideas sessions: lots of possible use cases, plenty of enthusiasm and no agreement on what to do first. An executive team needs more than a list. It needs to agree which use cases matter most to the business, what each one will cost and risk, how they will be governed and who owns the first 90 days.

That is a decision problem, not a design problem. Echelon's AI Design Sprint borrows the pace and structure of a sprint but is built around executive decisions: use cases scored against your strategic priorities, a governance framework and named owners for the first 90 days.

How to choose the right facilitator for a high-stakes session

Whichever format you choose, ask any facilitator these five questions before you book:

  1. What will we have in writing at the end of the day? Look for a specific document - a decision log, priority map or commitment register - not 'insights' or 'a summary'.
  2. How will you stop the most senior person from anchoring the room? A good answer mentions specific techniques, such as silent writing or independent ranking before discussion.
  3. What happens if we disagree? You want a facilitator who expects conflict and has a structured way of resolving it, not one who promises everyone will agree.
  4. What do you do before the session? Diagnostic calls and participant pre-work are signs that the session will be designed around your decisions rather than a standard agenda.
  5. What happens after? Ask how commitments are followed up. Decisions that are never checked tend to quietly unravel.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a design sprint and a strategy workshop?

A design sprint solves one defined problem by building and testing a prototype over several days. A strategy or decision workshop gets a leadership team to agree priorities, trade-offs and ownership, and produces a written record of those decisions rather than a prototype.

Can a design sprint be used for a leadership offsite?

Some sprint techniques work well at an offsite, such as silent writing, voting and time-boxing. But the full design sprint format assumes an agreed problem and a single decider, which leadership offsites often lack. Most offsites need a decision-focused design instead.

How long does a leadership decision workshop take?

Usually between half a day and two days. A half day suits a single focused decision, such as clarifying who decides what. A full day suits agreeing priorities across a leadership team, and one and a half to two days suits an annual strategy offsite.

Should we hire a facilitator or a consultancy for our strategy offsite?

Hire a consultancy if you need new analysis or research. Hire a specialist facilitator if your leaders already have the information and the problem is reaching agreement and following through on it.

What should a facilitated workshop produce?

For a leadership team, a written decision log is the minimum: each decision, the trade-off made, a named owner and a deadline. Good facilitators also provide a one-page summary of priorities and a plan for checking commitments after the session.
